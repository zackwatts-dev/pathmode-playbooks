### H1 doesn't hold: the markdown is a mechanical clearance price on discontinued equipment sold from branch stock, not a discount anyone chooses. The actionable leak is that two discontinued waves keep being restocked at standard cost and sold off the 42% ladder (-2.79M gross profit in 2025)

**$2.79 million a year** · confidence: medium · challenge: stands with changes

Nobody chooses the markdown. It appears only on equipment lines ordered on or after the SKU's discontinued_on date: 84,092 lines, against 0 among 2,635,247 other lines from 2016 to 2025 (q105, q96, q157). It follows a fixed ladder of 0.021 per month, capped at 0.42 (q114). No equipment SKU-day carries two different values (0 of 328,178, q120). The price is cut only when the order is filled from branch stock. In 2025, 99.2% of 8,623 stock lines came from a branch that held stock the day before, against 0.3% of 6,042 special lines (q212). Invoices match order prices on 100% of lines (q179). The markdown shows no lift in quote wins: difference-in-differences of -0.004, comparing 2022 with 2025 (q219, q221). So H1's reading, that rep or customer discounts failed to win volume, is ruled out. The issue the business can act on is narrower. The 2015 and 2023 discontinuation waves are still restocked at standard cost: 2,720 and 6,699 stock units bought in 2025 (q129). Both are sold from stock at the 0.42 cap. Gross profit on those marked-down stock lines in 2025 was -767,211 for the 2015 wave and -2,022,425 for the 2023 wave, -2,789,637 in total (q196, q217). The 2015 wave has run at about -0.77M a year since at least 2022. The whole rise comes from the 2023 wave, whose restocking dropped to 1,645 units in 2023 and then resumed at 6,163 (2024) and 6,699 (2025) (q196, q198). The headline -2.49M is a net figure. It includes +298,745 from the 2025 wave's pre-discontinuation stock, which is still low on the ladder (q217). Stopping restocking would avoid up to about 2.79M a year at 2025 volumes, if that demand simply goes away. The challenger proposed a higher bound of 4.78M, which assumes the stock demand moves to special orders at about 0.83 of list. The records don't support that bound. In each wave's first year after discontinuation, stock dried up and special orders fell rather than rose: 2,103 to 959 for the 2023 wave (2022 to 2023), and 2,112 to 651 for the 2025 wave (2024 to 2025) (q245, q246). Separately, 9,231 discontinued units are on hand at end-2025, 3,571 of them in the 2025 wave, which will reach the cap (q191, q221). A return-to-vendor channel at 0.80 of standard cost already exists (q185). Recommended action: purchasing should confirm why the 2015 and 2023 waves are still replenished for stock when the 2025 wave largely isn't, and cost selling at the cap against returning to vendor at 80%.

**Not evidence of:** This is not evidence of rep, branch or customer discretion. Price is set by SKU, date and whether the branch had stock, and customers rarely see both fulfilment paths: 65 of 5,301 customer-SKU pairs (2015 wave) and 227 of 9,460 (2023 wave) were filled both ways in 2025 (q211). Most of the 13.26M 'markdown dollars' is not money given away. markdown_pct sits on special, short and transfer lines whose price is not cut. That fits a clearance rule that applies only to stock on hand, and is probably not a keying error. It is a reporting overstatement. The vendor credits are not rebates on units sold: they are 80%-of-cost credits on returned units. The first-year drop in special orders is not proof that demand at 42% off would vanish if restocking stopped. Interpretation: the drop coincides with discontinuation itself, when customers may switch to replacement SKUs, so it is confounded. The branch correlation (n=12) is weak and partly mechanical.

**Couldn't tell:** Three questions remain open. (1) How much of the 42%-off stock demand would vanish, and how much would move to special orders at about 0.83 of list, if restocking stopped. This needs a controlled stop at some branches, or a SKU-successor mapping to see whether demand moves to replacements. (2) What discontinued_on means in practice. SKUs flagged in 2015 are still bought from vendors every year, which points to a pricing flag rather than vendor end-of-life. Pricing and purchasing would need to confirm. (3) Which customer orders the special POs serve, because POs carry no order_id. There is also no record of who or what triggers stock POs on discontinued SKUs.

**Next step:** Take the by-wave restocking pattern to purchasing. The 2025 wave's stock buys fell from 10,326 to 582 units, while the 2015 wave (2,720 units) and the 2023 wave (6,699 units) were still restocked in 2025. Ask whether that difference is a deliberate rule. Pilot a stop on stock replenishment for 2015- and 2023-wave SKUs at a few branches, and compare special-order and total volume against branches that keep restocking. That separates the 2.79M lower-bound saving from the demand question. Cost the 3,571 on-hand 2025-wave units at the 0.42 cap against vendor return at 0.80 of standard cost. Have markdown reporting count only stock-filled lines.

In sample for 2016-24: 1,707,089 excess across the slow and very slow tiers, worth 136,567 a year at 8% (85,354 at 5%). Very slow alone is 921,328, worth 73,706 a year. The released receivables are a one-time release of cash; only the carrying cost recurs. (q357, q343)

**Not evidence of:** Credit risk or bad debt: every 2016-24 invoice is paid in full. That terms or limits are too generous, or that tightening them would work: limits track neither size nor lateness, and there's no history of terms or limits. Why accounts pay late: credit memos and invoice-question contacts don't differ by tier, and there are no dispute or dunning records. That collections effort would recover the sized amount: the figures rest on an assumed rate and ignore lost sales. That any rep or staff member is responsible: rep variation is close to chance. Interpretation: flat tiers with no persistence inside them, no seasonality and identical attributes look more like how the records were generated than like real payer behaviour. Treat the tiers as the record as given, not as established behaviour.

**Couldn't tell:** Why these accounts pay late (disputes, deductions, cash-flow trouble, an informal agreement). Whether credit holds were ever applied, or whether credit limits are enforced. Whether early-pay discounts or late fees exist. How many sales tighter collections would cost. Answering these would take an AR adjustments ledger, collection activity logs, effective-dated credit terms and limits, and an order-to-collection history before and after any intervention.

**Next step:** Draw the very slow list from the most recent complete years with at least 10 invoices per customer (the q331 logic). Expect about a fifth of future overdue dollars to come from accounts not yet classifiable. Review the list with credit/AR for disputes or standing arrangements. Then randomise half the list to collection calls at due_day + 5 and keep half as a control. Measure the change in days late and in billing against the stable 31-day baseline. Confirm with finance whether credit limits are maintained or enforced.

## Looked at, not true

- **H8 does not hold: in a fixed-window, month-adjusted test, short lines and returns don't predict a customer stopping for 12 months (2017-2024); the 2016 excess is mostly timing, and order fade isn't a usable early warning:** Observed: 2017Q1-2024Q4 is a fixed-window test. Each customer-quarter's short lines are compared with what that customer's branch-month mix predicts. The outcome is no order in the next 4 quarters. Expected lapse is set within quarter x branch x trade x order band x line band (strata of 5 or more). Lapse runs at about expected in every band: 0.98 with no shorts (n=45,383), 1.059 under 1x expected, 1.078 at 1-2x, 1.042 at 2-4x and 1.028 at 4x+ (122 observed vs 118.7 expected, n=3,269) (q354). The window is not censored: 2025 has orders in all four quarters (q363). Returns show no link in either direction. Quarters with a damaged or wrong-item return: 1.111 (36 vs 32.4, n=1,665). Other returns only: 1.106 (50 vs 45.2, n=2,296). No return: 0.996 (n=69,930) (q361). In 2016 the same design shows a modest gradient, rising to 1.202 at 2x+ expected shorts (64 vs 53.3, n=1,140) (q356). That is far below the 127 vs 61.6 of the annual design (q292). Lapsers' orders fall in their final year: 0.5986 of the prior year, against 1.0563 for retained existing customers. They do not fall the year before: t-1 over t-2 was 1.086 for lapsers (n=1,758) vs 1.0493 for retained (n=16,538), median 1.0 in both (q359). Interpretation: there is no measurable link between short lines or returns and customers stopping. Most of the 2016 excess reflects when lapsing customers ordered. The 2016 residual could be a small real effect of the start-up stock-out, or noise. Lapse looks abrupt, not gradual. A first-half order-drop flag caught only 66 of 2,447 lapsers while they were still ordering (q362). The records support neither a service-recovery programme built on this link nor an order-fade watch list as an early warning.

## Data concerns

- Costs never change: every SKU has exactly one unit_cost across ten years in sales lines, PO lines and WMS, and it equals the product master (q21, q22, q68). Gross margin (27.29% in 2016 to 22.58% in 2025, q64) is therefore at standard cost, and the drift reflects price, not cost
- Vendor bills above the PO: 40,839 bills exceed PO value. 28,163 were paid in full (5,248,292.84 above PO value, including freight) and 12,026 were paid at PO value (2,249,435.22 withheld), q47, q48. Over-billing on qty or price appears at all 44 vendors in every year (q44). Receipts always equal PO qty, so billed qty above PO is qty above what was received (q42, q43)
- Duplicate vendor bills: 1,429 POs carry two bills with identical amounts and different vendor invoice numbers; 2,388 of the 2,858 bills are paid (q38, q40)
- Shipped but never invoiced: 5,932 shipped lines with no invoice line, steady at about 430-630 a year, worth for example 262,163.35 in 2016 and 271,314.82 in 2025 at order price (q14, q17). 1,430 orders shipped goods and have no invoice at all (q7, q9)
- Too clean to be real collections: no partial payments, no short pays, and no unpaid invoice older than about 85 operating days in ten years (q23, q25, q27). Bad debt and disputes are not recorded, or were handled outside these tables
- Return reasons are near-uniform over five codes (1,249-1,328 each), and the credit memo reason copies the return code, so it isn't independent evidence (q28, q30). 2 returns exceed the qty shipped (q30)
- Lost-quote reasons take only two values plus 19% blank, flat across years (q49, q51). Channel mix is almost identical every year for ten years (email 0.464-0.469 share, q63)
- Service notes are templated with no free text, and minutes fall in a fixed narrow band by channel (q55-q57). This looks system-generated rather than logged by people. No instructions or AI-addressed text found in notes, customer, product, vendor or rep names (q57, q59)
- Customer identity: 340 distinct names for 6,156 accounts, so possible duplicate accounts (q60, q61)
- Products sold outside their availability window: 84,465 lines ordered after the SKU's discontinued_on and 1,471 before available_from (q73). The meaning of these dates is undocumented
- WMS and ERP disagree on ship day by one day for 121,814 order-SKU pairs (q35). Opening balances exist for only 4,548 of 10,322 SKU-branch pairs, and 2016 has a short rate of 12.03% against 3-7% later, a likely start-up artefact (q64, q73)
- Vendor credits average 0.8 times standard cost, and 390 of 1,234 match no vendor return (q69). One transfer has qty 0 (q31, q62)
- 2025 end effects: 1,783 uninvoiced orders and 2,258 stock POs with no receipt (all expected after day 2520) sit in the final days, so 2025 is not comparable for completion measures (q5, q39)
- Orders in 2023 fell to 109,052 from 131,320 in 2022 while active customers rose (q63). Noted only, not investigated
- erp_branches.county contains city names, not counties, and housing_units are rounded figures (q66)

## What the records can't answer

- Profitability by customer, rep or branch at actual cost: costs are a fixed standard, and freight-in, rebates, labour and branch overhead aren't allocated. Needs a cost history, a vendor rebate ledger and a branch P&L
- Why customers return goods or why quotes are lost: the reason codes are coarse and undiscriminating. Needs free-text or audited reasons and competitor pricing
- Whether orders were keyed correctly: needs the original customer document (email, EDI or PO) to compare against the order lines
- Whether vendor bills were correct: needs vendor price lists or contracts and freight terms. The data can only show bills that differ from the PO and receipt
- What happened to short lines (backorder, substitution or lost sale): needs backorder linkage or lost-sales logging
- Bad debt, disputes and deductions: needs AR adjustment and write-off records
- Rep and staff effects over time: needs effective-dated rep assignments, hire and leave dates, and the person who took each service contact
- Which customer order a special PO serves, and the special-order lead time to the customer: needs an order_id on special POs, or a receipt for special goods
- Inventory accuracy and shrink: needs cycle counts and adjustment movements, none of which exist in the WMS
