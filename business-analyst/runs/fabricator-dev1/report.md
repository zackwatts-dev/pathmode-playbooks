# Where the money goes

**Narrowed: Wendlow absorbed the 2021 carbon-steel jump on repeat parts, about $1.09M a year over 2021-2022; prices have more than covered material since 2023, so no material gap remains to requote**
Repeat carbon-part prices lagged steel by about a year, leaving a net shortfall of $1,090,041 a year over 2021-2022. Prices have been above material cost since 2023, and the earlier $972,905-a-year "ongoing gap" is retracted. Confidence is medium. The next step is not to run a requote campaign on material grounds, but to put a steel index clause or a recorded surcharge line on repeat POs and to test whether post-2022 prices also cover labour.

**From 2017 to 2025, paid time not clocked to jobs grew by about 10,361 to 14,085 h a year (about $255,110 to $349,162 at 2025 rates) as estimated work fell while paid hours held, mostly in lathe, mill and laser. It is spread across whole roles, not a few employees, and it is an opportunity value of fixed payroll, not a cost that grew.**
Workload fell while paid hours held, leaving about 7.92 FTE of unclocked time across 8 roles, with no role above 1.73. This is worth $255,110 a year, and it becomes savings only if hours are matched to load. Confidence is medium. The next step is to introduce indirect clock codes for mill, lathe and laser and to review staffing against estimated hours through attrition and cross-training rather than reductions.

**Late payment costs about $1.41M in excess receivables and splits into three parts: invoices billed above the customer's PO price (22.67% of overdue dollar-days on 7.32% of invoices), customers 6 and 7, and a broad background tail on correctly priced invoices**
The $1,413,693 of excess receivables carries a cost of $113,095/yr at an assumed 8%; that is a carrying cost, not cash lost. Above-PO invoices are 65.28% over 30 days late against 16.74% for matching ones. Confidence is medium. The next steps are to check invoice price against PO price before invoicing, ask customers 6 and 7 about their agreed terms, and test a reminder at due date + 15 days.

**Wrong-revision scrap sits almost entirely on jobs that a new drawing revision missed: 99 of 137 reports are on 248 jobs where a revision arrived mid-job and no ECO was logged against the job. Release lag alone explains only 34. About $69,682/yr at cost, including fit/dimension/nonconforming credits**
At cost, scrap accounts for $39,195 a year and credits for $30,487, and the credits are an upper bound. Confidence is medium. The next step is that when a revision is received, every open job for that part gets an ECO or an explicit disposition. Release of new jobs should also be held while a received revision is unreleased.

**Mostly timing, not the person: quoter 115's lower new-quote hit rate is largely explained by quoting in months when estimators had just left. Matched by month, the gap is about 3 points, in line with a full-time estimator's. The bigger lever is estimating coverage and quote turnaround.**
The shortfall is $20,872 a year of quoted revenue, not margin, and most of it is not recoverable. Faster quotes go with more wins. Confidence is medium. The next step is to track turnaround and who sent each quote, plan backfill or cross-training before an estimator leaves, and record assignment and loss reasons.

**Scrap is not concentrated in fixable parts, families, first runs or people once volume and routing are allowed for; the one robust pattern is a first-year learning curve (about 1.8x the tenured rate), worth roughly $18,900 a year at cost**
First-year staff scrap at 1.804x the tenured rate, falling to about the tenured rate within the same people. That excess is worth $18,914 a year at cost, against an upper bound of $54,971 a year at selling price. Confidence is medium. The next step is to trial first-piece verification and paired setups for new hires and to track each hire's total scrap per piece over 12 months.

**No large customer loses money under any cost allocation tested; whether low-dollar family 1 work loses money depends on how indirect labour is charged, which the records cannot settle**
On the hour basis, 22 repeat parts fall below zero, worth $8,295.4 a year, but this is not a recoverable loss. Confidence is medium. The next step is to sample what indirect staff spend time on per job before choosing an allocation. Separately, the 217 completed but uninvoiced jobs should be reviewed.

**Late material is linked to about 2.8 times the late-shipment rate at the same planned slack, but it does not explain the 2016 and 2021 spikes. 2016 was a delay inside the plant. About four tenths of the 2021 excess came from vendor lead times that roughly doubled**
This amounts to about 81.4 extra late shipments a year, and no dollar figure is supported. Confidence is medium. The next steps are to compare operation timing in Sep-Dec 2016 and Mar-Oct 2021 with 2019, and to measure material against the job's need date as well as the PO due date.

Several things were tested and found not to hold:
- The ongoing carbon material gap of $972,905 a year was retracted.
- Scrap does not concentrate in particular parts, families, first runs or employees once volume and routing are allowed for.
- Quoter 115's gap shrinks to -0.031 once quotes are matched by month.
- No customer with at least $500k of revenue loses money, and the earlier ranking of small-quantity work as weakest came from the per-hour rule.
- The unadjusted ninefold late-material effect (8.96x) fell to 2.83x once planned slack was matched.
- Unclocked time is not a cost that grew.

The records cannot answer several questions:
- Why prices were held through 2021.
- Whether total margin, including labour and overhead, has recovered.
- What unclocked hours were spent on, and whether the 2016 drop in clocked hours was efficiency or a change in booking practice.
- Why above-PO invoices are paid late, and whether customers 6 and 7 have terms other than the recorded 30 days.
- How indirect labour should be allocated.
- Why quotes were lost.
- Which work centre slowed jobs in 2016 and 2021.
- Whether the floor worked from old prints.

## Findings, by dollars a year

### Narrowed: Wendlow absorbed the 2021 carbon-steel jump on repeat parts, about $1.09M a year over 2021-2022; prices have more than covered material since 2023, so no material gap remains to requote

**$1.09 million a year** · confidence: medium · challenge: stands with changes

Observed: on the same carbon parts at their 2020 shipped quantities, the price index in 2021 was 1.0007 while material per unit was 1.5475 times 2020, so margin over material fell $2,077,947 across 1,686 parts (q176). The rise in material per unit on those parts came from $/lb, not weight: the pounds index stayed between 0.997 and 1.0087 (q176), and carbon cost $0.3123/lb in 2020, $0.4857 in 2021 and $0.5959 in 2022 (q173). Prices reset in 2022 (index 1.2271), and margin over material on the matched parts was about even that year (+$166,479). It was above 2020 from 2023 on: +$1,526,003 in 2023, +$1,312,006 in 2024 and +$949,945 in 2025 (q176). The result holds when 2019 is the base year instead: -$1,358,121 in 2021, then +$958,869 to +$1,364,746 a year in 2023-2025 (q215). Across all carbon order jobs, steel cost added versus 2020 $/lb exceeded the estimated price gain by $2,913,379 in 2021. Prices gained $733,298 more than steel cost in 2022, which leaves a net shortfall of $1,090,041 a year over 2021-2022 (q188, q213). Interpretation: repeat-part prices lagged steel by about a year. Material's share of price (about 0.29-0.31 in 2024-2025 against about 0.25 in 2020; q173, q176) stays higher because prices passed steel through dollar for dollar, not because cost is going unrecovered. My earlier $972,905-a-year ongoing gap was produced by measuring material against a fixed share, and I retract it. Action: a steel-indexed price clause, or a surcharge line recorded on repeat POs, so that the next steel spike reaches prices within a quarter rather than a year.

- Matched carbon parts at 2020 quantities: in 2021, price index 1.0007 and material index 1.5475, margin over material -$2,077,947 (1,686 parts). In 2022, price 1.2271, material 1.9243, +$166,479 (1,305 parts). In 2023-2025, +$1,526,003, +$1,312,006 and +$949,945 (1,012, 804 and 643 parts). (q176)

- The 2021-2022 squeeze was not concentrated in one segment or customer: carbon share rose in all five segments, and the top 10 customers held 0.6356 of the excess against 0.6523 of revenue. (q140, q149)

**Not evidence of:** This measures margin over material only. Labour, outside processing and overhead are not in it. The hours-weighted pay rate rose by a factor of 1.2328 from 2020 to 2025 (q193, q195), so the over-recovery on material since 2023 does not show that total margin is back to 2020 levels. The all-jobs figure (q188, q213) applies the price index of the surviving matched parts to all carbon work, which is an assumption. The matched set shrinks from 2,395 parts to 643 (q176). It does not show that steel caused the price changes (they coincide in time only), or that the invoice uplift is a steel surcharge, although the surcharge flag matches some of it (q184).

**Couldn't tell:** Why prices were held through 2021 (contract, blanket PO, or a choice to keep volume). Whether parts that dropped out after the 2022 reset were lost because of the reset. Labour hours and overhead per job, which would show whether total contribution has recovered. What the invoice uplift on untraced repeat orders is.

**Next step:** Do not run a requote campaign on carbon repeat parts on material grounds; the records show no current material gap. Put a steel index clause or a recorded surcharge line on repeat POs so that recovery lag can be measured next time. Test whether the post-2022 price level covers labour as well, using shop labour hours per unit on matched parts. Correct the map: outside-processing PO lines carry $1,718,008 of amount (q101), not $0.

### From 2017 to 2025, paid time not clocked to jobs grew by about 10,361 to 14,085 h a year (about $255,110 to $349,162 at 2025 rates) as estimated work fell while paid hours held, mostly in lathe, mill and laser. It is spread across whole roles, not a few employees, and it is an opportunity value of fixed payroll, not a cost that grew.

**$255,110 a year** · confidence: medium · challenge: stands with changes

The hypothesis holds in a narrower form. Total paid hours barely moved from 2016 to 2025 (+511.3 h, q391), and annual clocked hours are labour on operations completed that year, with little truncation at the end of the series (q371, q372, q373, q375). The original 2016 baseline overstated the trend. During 2016, clocked hours per estimated hour fell from about 1.0 to about 0.81-0.88 (e.g. laser 0.992 in Jan-2016 to 0.811 in Dec-2016; q374), on the same parts with unchanged estimates (q389). 58.5% of the 2016-2025 fall in clocked hours comes from that ratio effect (q385, q391). Measured from 2017, the picture is cleaner: estimated work on completed operations fell (load effect -14,314 clocked-hour equivalents), while clocked hours per estimated hour held or rose slightly (ratio effect +2,342; shop-wide 0.883 in 2017, 0.903 in 2025; q383, q385). Unclocked paid time grew by 14,085 h (2017 vs 2025), or by 10,361 h a year comparing the 2017-19 and 2023-25 averages (q379, q395). Each role books only to its own work centre (q382). The rise moves the whole within-role distribution (q349). The largest pieces against 2017 are lathe (1.73 FTE-equivalents), mill (1.64) and laser (1.49) (q390). Interpretation: capacity did not follow a falling workload. In mill and lathe, load fell while paid hours were held (mill -104 h) or added (lathe +1,272 h). Laser lost load while paid hours rose 1,896 h, partly by deliberate backlog hiring (2 backlog requisitions, 2016-08-01 and 2021-01-25; q386). Priced at 2025 median rates, this is $255,110 a year on 3-year averages, of which mill plus lathe is $116,772 (q395). The 2017 baseline gives $349,162 (q379). It is an opportunity value of hours already paid: about 7.92 FTE spread across 8 roles, with none above 1.73 (q390). It becomes savings only by matching hours to load through attrition, cross-training or bringing in work. The 2016 step is a separate question: it could be real efficiency or labour no longer booked to jobs, and the records can't tell which.

- Annual clocked hours are a sound measure. Between 96.5% (2016) and 98.0% (2025) of labour entries are dated on their operation's completion date, with 0 completion-year mismatches. Every operation completed 2016-2025 has labour, and open operations at the end of 2025 hold only 43.2 h of labour. Timecards cover 49-50 weeks a year. (q370, q371, q372, q373, q375)
- 2016 is a transition year. Act/est fell within 2016 (laser 0.992 to 0.811, paint 1.02 to 0.878, brake 1.069 to 0.864, Jan to Dec), on identical parts whose estimates per piece were essentially unchanged by 2018 (median ratio 1.01-1.019, 409-941 parts per work centre), while clocked hours per piece fell (median 0.761-0.858). Of the 15,739 h fall in clocked hours 2016-2025, 9,209 h (58.5%) is this ratio effect. (q374, q389, q385, q391)
- From 2017 the fall in clocked hours is a workload fall: load effect -14,314 h, ratio effect +2,342 h (shop-wide act/est 0.883 in 2017, 0.903 in 2025). (q385, q383)
- Unclocked paid time grew by 14,085 h (2025 vs 2017) or 10,361 h a year (2023-25 vs 2017-19 averages). Against 2017: lathe +3,269 h (paid +1,272, clocked -1,997), mill +2,832 h (paid -104, clocked -2,936), laser +2,823 h (paid +1,896, clocked -927), brake +1,672 h, assembler +1,435 h, painter +1,303 h (paid only +213), welder +442 h, inspector +308 h. (q379, q390)
- The shift is role-wide, not driven by a few people. For example, mill machinists' median unclocked share went 0.366 (2016, n=16) to 0.561 (2025, n=15), and laser operators' p10 went -0.118 to 0.188. Each role books 100% of its labour to its own work centre. (q349, q382)
- Priced at 2025 median role rates: $255,110 a year on 3-year averages (mill $63,079, laser $63,772, lathe $53,693, mill plus lathe $116,772) and $349,162 on a 2017 baseline. That equals 7.92 FTE across 8 roles, at most 1.73 in any one role. (q395, q379, q390)

**Not evidence of:** Not evidence that any employee is idle or under-recording: whole roles move, and individual shares below zero in 2016 (q349) show that attributing hours to people is imperfect. Not evidence that a recording change happened in 2016, or that estimates were right; the 2016 drop could be genuine efficiency. Not a cost that grew: payroll changed by +511.3 h (q391), and the slack is under 2 FTE per role (q390). Not evidence of what the unclocked time was spent on.

**Couldn't tell:** What the unclocked hours were spent on: there are no indirect clock codes. Whether the 2016 fall in clocked hours per piece was efficiency or a change in booking practice. Why lathe and inspector shares stay high in every year. Whether unclocked time rises in low-load weeks: work_date is effectively the completion date, so weekly load can't be measured reliably. Role changes over time: hr_employees holds only the current role. Benefits are excluded from the dollars.

**Next step:** Introduce indirect clock codes for mill, lathe and laser, and review staffing in those centres against estimated hours, using attrition (mill and lathe each had 18 replacement requisitions, q386) and cross-training rather than reductions. Ask the plant and accounting whether labour-booking practice changed during 2016 before treating that step as capacity.

### Wrong-revision scrap sits almost entirely on jobs that a new drawing revision missed: 99 of 137 reports are on 248 jobs where a revision arrived mid-job and no ECO was logged against the job. **Not evidence of:** Not evidence that late receipts cause late shipments. Even the slack-matched 81.4 a year is an association: rushed orders could produce both short PO due dates and late vendors. Not evidence that 2021 material supply was fine: 'on time' was measured against PO due dates that had lengthened. Not evidence of which work centre held jobs. Release-to-completion time is a weak measure of flow: it fell over the decade from 21.4 days (2016) to 8.6 (2025), and 2017 ran 18.4 days with a late rate of only 0.0307 (q344). Not evidence about vendors whose receipts are often unrecorded. The unreceived share is 0.4416 for vendor 1, 0.3881 for aluminium, 0.4274 for stainless and 1.0 for both outside processors (q338). Outside-processing jobs ran late 0.6602 (2016) and 0.6807 (2021) and can never carry a late-receipt flag (q341). Not evidence that capacity was adequate: clocked hours are posting-dated and measure neither skill nor queues.

**Couldn't tell:** Which operation or work centre slowed jobs at the same slack in 2016 and 2021: operations carry only completion dates. Whether vendor lead times rose in 2021 because vendors quoted longer or because Wendlow set later due dates: POs record only ordered and due dates. Whether unreceived POs (flat at 0.2884-0.3115 a year, q337) were cancelled or received without a record. Jobs with an unreceived PO and no recorded late receipt ran late 0.0521, against 0.0331 for fully received jobs (q336), so some late material is probably hidden there. The 428 order jobs promised in 2016 with no PO (late 0.1262, q336) reflect PO data starting 2016-01-05 while releases start 2015-11-27 (q335). They slightly inflate early-2016 rates but not the Sep-Dec spike. No dollar figure is supported: direct cost is small and expedited freight is billed to customers (prior q76, q82, q98).

## Looked at, not true

- **Unreceived material POs are not duplicates: all 30,888 are either one scrap-replacement buy per scrap report (30,802, $2,366,230) or Dec-2025 orders not yet due (86). Whether the replacement buys were ever filled can't be told from these records:** The hypothesis does not hold. The 2016-2025 records account for every one of the 30,888 unreceived material POs ($2,489,397; q318, q321). 30,802 are scrap-replacement buys, exactly one per scrap report: 30,652 of the 30,670 follow-up POs are dated the same day as the matching scrap report (q237), and all 149 unreceived POs on jobs carried in from before 2016 match a same-day scrap report (q288). They are sized to the scrapped pieces (median pound ratio 0.994, p10-p90 0.917-1.07; q239) and priced like the job's first PO (median price-per-lb ratio 1.0026; q310). Together they total $2,366,230, or $236,623/yr and 2.6% of GL material (q310). The other 86 were ordered 2025-12-11 to 12-31 and total $123,167 (q246, q321). Only 2 of those were past due at 2025-12-31, by at most 9 days (q306). No duplicates were found. Within a job, only 304 of 30,888 unreceived POs are within 10% of a received PO's pounds (q228). Across jobs, exact twins are no more common within 0-5 days than within 30-35 days (3,207 vs 3,863; q304). What is open is whether the replacement material came. The ledger books material when the PO is placed (GL material and GL outside processing match PO dollars in all 120 months; q277), so the GL can't show it. Rework labour starts a median 0 days after the replacement PO is placed, and before the PO is due on 24,314 of 25,274 jobs (96.2%; q272, q321). So shipping in full doesn't show that the PO material arrived. Interpretation: the re-runs were probably cut from stock, and the replacement PO is either restocking or was never filled. If it was never filled, GL material is overstated by up to $2,366,230 over 10 years. Separately, outside-processing POs are never receipted (1,234 POs, $1,718,008, one per job; q314, q318). That is a recording convention, not a sign of duplication. Action: pull vendor invoices/AP for a sample of replacement POs from vendors 1, 3 and 4. Have replacement buys receipted or tagged. Pursue the cost through scrap reduction.
- **H4 does not hold. New-part quotes are not over-estimated: first jobs run at or above quoted hours, weld at 1.267x excluding rework. Quotes with more hours than their peers are not lost more often. The low shop-wide actual/estimate ratios come from repeat runs of the same parts getting faster on routings that are never updated.:** The hypothesis fails on both legs. Observed: this covers all won new quotes, 2,764 orders out of 2,764 won new quotes. Every won new quote has an order and no other quote does (q289). That is 5,390 completed jobs, every one of them the first job for its part (q292), from quote years 2016-2025. On each of them the job routing copies the quote exactly. Actual hours excluding rework meet or exceed quoted hours at every quoted work centre except lathe: laser 1.006 (5,118 ops), brake 1.013 (3,788), mill 1.042 (333), paint 1.061 (3,663), assembly 1.125 (1,043), weld 1.267 (2,373), and lathe 0.935 (117) (q301). There is no duplication from the joins. Inspect is never quoted and runs at 1.06 against its routing (q303). Rework is 0-1.09% of hours by work centre (q295). On the hit-rate side, new quote lines were compared with peers of the same part family, material, quantity decile and year (21,851 lines). Lines that carry more hours than their peers win at 0.2442-0.253 across quintiles, which is flat. Hit rate does fall with relative price, from 0.2624 to 0.2288 (q313). Among quotes where the buyer actually decided (won vs lost, excluding expired), the labour-heavy quintiles win more often, not less: 0.4865 in the lowest hours-per-material quintile vs 0.5597-0.5794 in the rest (q332). The low shop-wide actual/estimate ratios come from repeats. In a fixed panel of parts with 11+ jobs, laser falls from 0.925 on the part's first job to 0.736 on its 11th and later, brake from 0.972 to 0.733, paint from 1.024 to 0.783 and weld from 1.181 to 0.909. Over the same jobs inspect stays at 1.053-1.066, average quantities are flat, and setup and run-per-piece estimates stay within 1.000-1.033x of the first job (q322). The ordering holds within every creation year 2016-2025 (q326) and on true first runs of parts created from 2017 on (q324). Interpretation: this is consistent with learning on routings that are set once and never updated. The records can't show the mechanism. Two things are worth acting on instead of padded hours: weld is under-quoted on new parts, and repeat-part routings stay at first-run hours.

## Data concerns

- Overtime stops completely in 2025: zero overtime hours in labour, timecards and GL memo and zero authorisations, after 320-5,095 h/yr in 2016-2024 (q38). This could be a policy change or a recording gap; the records can't tell which.
- GL memo accounts are missing months instead of showing zero: rework labour is present in 101 of 120 months, overtime in 61 (q3, q38).
- Labour entry 'hours' are not daily: 8,078 entries are over 24 h, the largest 208.25 h, and one employee-date totals 229.7 h (q40, q42, q74). work_date can't be used to measure daily load. 7 entries have hours <= 0 (q39).
- Scrap is recorded where it was found, not where it was caused: weld defects are never booked at weld, and wrong-revision scrap shows up at paint, assembly and brake (q46). Scrap reports total 46,931 pieces against 36,362 on labour entries; 1,186 ops have a scrap report with no labour scrap, concentrated in weld defect, paint and wrong revision (q43, q45, q74).
- The scrap note field is blank on all 30,802 rows (q5). Credit memo reason is 'return' on all 81 rows (q3). Termination reason is 'resigned' for 209 of 210 leavers (q3). These columns barely vary.
- First-article inspections (967) all have a null inspector_id (q9).
- Engineering revision turnaround looks too uniform: received-to-released p90 is exactly 5.0 days in every year 2016-2025, with recv->log 1.5-1.6 and log->release 2.1-2.3 every year (q28).
- Invoices exceed qty x PO price by more than 0.5% on 5,115 of 77,077 invoices (max ratio 1.787). None before 2018-07-12; they cluster in 2018-2019 and 2021-2022 and almost vanish by 2024-2025 (q20, q21). No column says what the extra charge is. [Interpretation, untested: it looks like a material surcharge, given the quote_lines surcharge flag on 9,444 lines (q52).]
- 28.9% of material POs have no receipt (28.0%-30.2% by year, q49, q74), yet 76,049 of 76,730 jobs have at least one received PO (q50); the GL books those unreceived POs as material cost (q59). Cancelled or duplicate POs can't be identified.
- Outside-processing POs carry 0 lb and $0 (q48) and never have receipts (q47). Outside cost exists only as a GL total, so it can't be attributed to jobs.
- Late shipments vs promise (960 in 2016, 1,384 in 2021) coincide with spikes in chase emails (898 and 1,160) and overtime (q38, q51, q66). This is co-occurrence only.
- 2021 also shows fewer quotes (1,971) and fewer orders carrying a quote_id (193) (q11), along with the most hires (36) and the longest time to fill (152.0 days in 2021, 162.4 in 2022) (q69).
- Customer part_number is not unique per customer: 200 duplicate groups with different descriptions (q72, q73). Use part_id.
- Customer PO numbers repeat across orders, up to 7 times (q65).
- Edge effects at the ends of the data: 504 orders and their jobs date from Nov-Dec 2015, before the calendar starts (q1, q11). At the end, 287 jobs are incomplete, 106 unreleased, 70 quotes have no status (q2, q3) and 287 lines from 2025 are unshipped (q51). Invoice due_on is capped at 2025-12-31 (764 invoices, q16, q74).
- Timecard week_of falls on a Thursday, Friday or Wednesday for 4,251 rows around holidays; it is a 5-working-day block, not a calendar week (q35, q36, q74).
- QCEW 332313 shows pay of 0 in suppressed rows (8 of 10 flagged). Treat these as missing, not zero (q68).
- Headcount: HR shows 131-141 active at year end, but only 95-103 employees have timecards and 94-104 have labour entries per year (q37). The owner's 'about 150' presumably includes salaried staff and isn't reconciled here.
- No text in the records addresses an AI or gives instructions: searched mail subjects, part descriptions, customer names and RFQ buyers (q7, q8).

## What the records can't answer

- Why a quote was lost: status is only what the buyer reported, 'expired' means silence, and there is no loss reason or competitor price. Would need win/loss reasons captured at status entry.
- Which work centre made a job late: operations have completion dates but no start or queue times, and labour work_date is a posting date (q42). Would need operation start/stop timestamps or queue/dispatch records.
- Whether an estimate was right, directly: quote hours are per piece and job hours are job totals, and quotes link to orders by key for only 2,764 orders (q9, q53, q54). This can only be approximated by matching on customer+part, and it ignores the quantity actually run.
- Whether a part was made to the wrong revision: no record of the revision actually machined. revision_issued can be compared with released revisions (32,214 of 32,372 jobs match, q26), and 'wrong revision' scrap is self-reported (137 reports, q4). Would need revision verification at first article or final inspection.
- Where a defect originated, as opposed to where it was found (q46). Would need cause-operation coding on scrap reports.
- Job- or customer-level margin including outside processing and freight allocation: outside cost is not on POs (q48), and GL revenue doesn't tie to invoices monthly (q61).
- What the invoice uplift over PO price is for (q20). Would need invoice line detail.
- What happened to unreceived POs (q49). Would need a PO status/cancellation field.
- Indirect labour by activity (40,400-59,559 paid hours/yr not clocked to jobs, q74). Would need indirect clock codes.
- Email content (RFQ specs, expedite reasons): only metadata is held.
